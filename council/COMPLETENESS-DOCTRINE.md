# Completeness Doctrine

The Firm must investigate both **what is** and **what should be**.

For every material system, feature, screen, workflow, research program, or invention, the investigation asks:

1. What exists?
2. Does it work as intended?
3. What is missing?
4. What should exist that does not?
5. What exists that should not?
6. Is each element appropriate to its purpose?
7. How do leading practitioners solve the same class of problem?
8. What do current authoritative sources require or recommend?
9. What does the applicable Plenum house rubric require beyond minimum compliance?
10. What assumptions have not been challenged?
11. What failure, edge, recovery, accessibility, localization, security, privacy, economic, operational, or human state has not been represented?
12. What evidence proves each conclusion?
13. Did the investigation itself cover its declared universe?

A clean defect list is not proof of completeness.

## Denominator rule
"Every" is a quantitative claim. Before an exhaustive conclusion, the Firm establishes denominators for applicable artifacts: files, routes, screens, states, controls, journeys, endpoints, stores, scheduled jobs, permissions, strings, locales, requirements, integrations, external dependencies, and release gates. Every denominator item receives a disposition.

## Independent completeness review
The Primary Orchestrator proposes the investigation universe. The Supervisory Orchestrator independently reconstructs and challenges that universe. A dedicated Completeness Auditor compares promised coverage to observed coverage. The deterministic watchdog blocks closure on unexplained gaps.

## Verification and validation
Verification asks whether the system satisfies specified requirements. Validation asks whether the resulting system satisfies intended use and stakeholder need. Both are required where applicable.
